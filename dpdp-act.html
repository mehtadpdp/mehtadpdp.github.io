Understanding the Law

The DPDP Act, 2023 & the DPDP Rules, 2025

Everything an organisation needs to know about what's now law, when it bites, and what it costs to get wrong.

Overview

A staggered commencement, with a hard deadline

The DPDP Act, 2023 has been on the books for a while, but it only started to bite once the DPDP Rules were notified on 13 November 2025 (G.S.R. 846(E)). That notification switched the law on in stages rather than all at once, and the Data Protection Board of India — the body that will actually enforce it — is now up and running.

The obligations that matter most day to day — how you take consent, the security you're required to keep in place, how fast you must report a breach, and how long you're allowed to hold someone's data — all become mandatory on 13 May 2027. There's no grace period built in after that date.

That sounds like a long runway, but it isn't. Getting an organisation genuinely ready — finding the data, fixing the gaps, and making the new habits stick — routinely takes the better part of a year, sometimes longer. And the law doesn't stop at India's borders: a company based anywhere in the world is covered the moment it offers goods or services to people in India. This is fundamentally a Board-level governance question, not something that can be handed entirely to IT.

Who Must Comply

  • Any Data Fiduciary — meaning any organisation that decides why and how digital personal data gets processed, whether it does so alone or jointly.
  • Any Data Processor working on a Fiduciary's behalf — Section 8(2) keeps liability with the Fiduciary no matter what the vendor contract promises.
  • Foreign entities too, wherever they are based, if they offer goods or services to people in India (Section 3).
  • Firms designated Significant Data Fiduciaries under Section 10 face a heavier compliance load under Rule 13.
Key Dates

The Compliance Timeline

13 Nov 2025

13 Nov 2026

13 May 2027

In Detail

The Act, Section by Section

Five groups of obligations, from the initial diagnostic through to what happens once you're designated a Significant Data Fiduciary. Expand each to see what it actually involves.

A

Readiness & Gap Assessment

Ten workstreams that establish where you stand before 13 May 2027
+

01  Applicability & Scoping Assessment

  • Working out whether an organisation is a Data Fiduciary, a Data Processor, or both — and which processing activities actually fall under the Act.
  • Checking exposure under Section 3, the extra-territorial provision that pulls in any entity outside India offering goods or services to people here.
  • Seeing how DPDP sits alongside obligations already owed to RBI, SEBI, IRDAI or TRAI, and the IT Act / SPDI Rules that remain in force until DPDP's core provisions take effect.
  • Flagging early whether an entity is likely to be designated a Significant Data Fiduciary under Section 10.

02  Data Discovery, Inventory & Mapping

You can't protect data you haven't found — this is the groundwork everything else rests on.

  • Building a genuinely complete inventory of personal data across systems, applications, vendors and the odd physical record that's never made it into a spreadsheet.
  • Mapping how that data actually moves — collection, processing, storage, sharing, transfer, disposal.
  • Calling out children's data, data belonging to persons with disabilities, and any other high-risk processing along the way.

03  Gap Assessment & Compliance Diagnostic

A line-by-line check against what the Act and Rules actually require.

  • Testing current practice against every operative obligation in the DPDP Act and Rules.
  • Reading through existing privacy notices, consent flows, policies, contracts and security controls.
  • Producing a gap register with each item risk-rated, so it's clear what's urgent and what can wait.
  • A prioritised remediation roadmap, not just a list of problems.

04  Notice & Consent Architecture

Most existing consent flows won't clear the bar the Rules now set.

  • Standalone, plain-language privacy notices, in English and the languages listed in the Eighth Schedule.
  • Checking that consent is actually free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, captured through a clear affirmative action rather than a pre-ticked box.
  • Making withdrawal genuinely as easy as giving consent, and working out how to re-consent people whose data is already held.
  • The legitimate-use grounds under Section 7, for cases where consent isn't the basis at all.

05  Data Principal Rights & Grievance Redressal

Turning Sections 11–14 and Rule 14 into something a support team can actually run.

  • How requests for access, correction, completion, updating, erasure and nomination actually get handled end to end.
  • A grievance process that closes complaints within the timelines the law sets.

06  Security Safeguards & Breach Readiness

This is where the biggest penalties live — Rule 6 on safeguards and Rule 7's breach clock.

  • The minimum safeguards the Rules expect: encryption, obfuscation, masking or tokenisation, access control, ongoing monitoring, and continuity planning.
  • A breach-response playbook that gets word to affected people "without delay" and a full report to the Board inside 72 hours of the organisation becoming aware.
  • There's no materiality threshold — every breach is reportable, however small it looks.

07  Retention, Erasure & Logging

Rule 8, read together with the Third Schedule.

  • Retention periods tied to the actual purpose data was collected for, with erasure that triggers automatically once consent is withdrawn or the purpose is served.

08  Children's & Disability Data

Section 9 and the Fourth Schedule — up to ₹200 crore in exposure if this goes wrong.

  • How parental consent is verified and how age is assured in practice.
  • The ban on tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted advertising aimed at children.
  • Consent from a lawful guardian, properly obtained for persons with disabilities.

09  Processor & Vendor Contract Management

Section 8(2) means liability for a processor's mistake doesn't disappear just because the work was outsourced.

  • Reviewing and redlining processor agreements, cloud contracts and outsourcing arrangements.
  • DPDP-ready data processing addenda covering safeguards, sub-processing, breach reporting, audit rights, and what happens to the data on exit.
  • Vendor risk assessments and due-diligence questionnaires.
  • Approval protocols for onward transfers and sub-processors.

10  Cross-Border Transfer & Sectoral Overlay

Section 16 works on a negative list — but a sectoral regulator may still be stricter.

  • Transfers outside India and the restrictions that apply to specific notified countries.
  • Localisation rules that survive independently of DPDP — RBI payment-data localisation, the Digital Lending Directions, and IRDAI or SEBI requirements.
  • The extra localisation duties that apply once an entity is a Significant Data Fiduciary under Rule 13(4).
  • Transfer-impact assessments and contractual protections for offshore group entities and vendors.

Typical Outputs

  • DPDP Applicability & Scoping Note
  • Data Inventory
  • Risk-rated Gap Assessment Report
  • Remediation Roadmap
B

Implementation & Documentation

The paper trail an auditor or the Board will actually ask to see
+

Under DPDP, compliance is only as real as the paperwork behind it. A complete programme typically runs to the following documents:

  • Privacy Policy and Data Protection Policy
  • Standalone privacy notices and consent artefacts (web, app, onboarding, offline-to-digital)
  • Record of Processing Activities (RoPA) and data-flow maps
  • Data Retention and Erasure Policy with retention schedule
  • Incident Response and Breach Notification Plan, with Board and Data Principal intimation templates
  • Data Principal Rights Request Handling Procedure and grievance-redressal charter
  • Data Processing Agreements and vendor addenda
  • Children's Data and Age-Assurance Policy
  • Cross-Border Transfer Policy and transfer register
  • DPIA framework, templates and completed assessments
  • Access Control, Encryption and Logging Standards
  • Employee awareness training modules and attendance records

A Note on Evidence

  • It's the Data Fiduciary's job to prove compliance — not the regulator's job to disprove it.
  • A consent record that can't actually be retrieved when asked is, for all practical purposes, no consent at all.
  • Documentation should be built to be produced on demand, not filed away and forgotten.
C

Significant Data Fiduciary Obligations

What Rule 13 adds once an entity is notified under Section 10
+

Data Protection Impact Assessment — Rule 13(1), every twelve months

  • A structured DPIA method built for high-risk, large-scale processing
  • Assessing risk to Data Principals and designing ways to mitigate it
  • Reporting significant DPIA findings to the Board

Annual Data Audit — an independent audit, once a year

  • Scoping and running a DPDP compliance audit across the full data lifecycle
  • Appointing and coordinating an Independent Data Auditor
  • Preparing the audit report and its findings for the Board

Data Protection Officer

  • Appointing a DPO based in India who reports to the Board
  • Defining the DPO's mandate, reporting lines and authority to escalate
  • Publishing the DPO's contact details as the point of contact for grievances

Algorithmic Due Diligence — Rule 13(2)

  • Checking that the algorithms behind hosting, display, uploads, edits or sharing of personal data don't put Data Principals at risk
  • Documenting that check in a form regulators can review

Governance & Board Reporting

  • The privacy governance structure and internal escalation path
  • Keeping the Board and Audit Committee briefed on DPDP compliance status
  • An evidence trail that shows the Board is actually accountable, not just aware
D

Staying Compliant, Year on Year

Why DPDP compliance doesn't end when a project closes
+

New products, new vendors and new processing activities all reopen compliance questions, which is why this tends to run as an ongoing mandate rather than a one-time exercise:

Periodic Compliance Management

  • The annual DPIA and independent audit for Significant Data Fiduciaries
  • Reviewing notices, consent flows and the RoPA as products and processing evolve
  • Checking retention and erasure cycles against the logs
  • Refreshing vendor and processor contracts and re-running vendor risk checks

Rights & Grievance Desk

  • Logging and tracking rights requests against statutory deadlines
  • Keeping the grievance register current and closing complaints inside the 90-day window
  • Handling escalations and any responses the Data Protection Board requires

Breach Response Support

  • On-call help classifying a breach and issuing the "without delay" notice to affected people
  • Preparing and filing the detailed 72-hour report to the Board
  • Documenting root cause, remedial steps and follow-up filings
  • Coordinating with CERT-In where its separate six-hour reporting window also applies

Training & Awareness

  • Role-based training for engineering, marketing, HR, support and vendor-management teams
  • Briefing the Board and senior management on personal liability and governance exposure
  • Annual refreshers with assessments and documented completion for the record

Regulatory Watch & Board Reporting

  • Tracking Board orders, MeitY notifications, SDF designations and sector-specific guidance
  • Quarterly compliance dashboards and a management compliance certificate
  • Flagging the November 2026 and May 2027 activation dates well ahead of time

Annual DPDP Health Check

  • Consent and notice integrity, and the lawful basis behind every processing activity
  • Security safeguards, logging and breach preparedness
  • Processor contract coverage and the cross-border transfer register
  • The overall governance framework and whether the evidence trail would hold up to audit
E

Where DPDP Meets Everything Else

Sectoral overlays, deal-making and specialised risk review
+

DPDP rarely sits in isolation — it interacts with other regulation and with commercial deals. The situations that come up most often beyond the core obligations above:

  • Sector-specific DPDP overlays for NBFCs and digital lenders (RBI Digital Lending Directions), banking, insurance, healthcare, ed-tech and e-commerce
  • Privacy due diligence in M&A, investment and outsourcing deals
  • GDPR-to-DPDP bridge assessments for multinational groups harmonising a global privacy programme
  • Engaging an Independent Data Auditor and defending the resulting audit
  • Representation before, and responses to, the Data Protection Board of India
  • Privacy-by-design review for new products, features and data-monetisation ideas
  • AI and algorithmic-processing risk review under Rule 13(2)
The Cost of Getting It Wrong

Penalties Under the Schedule to the Act

Penalties are assessed per contravention, and a single incident can trigger more than one of the heads below at the same time — so the numbers stack.

Maximum PenaltyContravention
₹250 croreFailure to take reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach
₹200 croreFailure to notify the Board or affected Data Principals of a personal data breach
₹200 croreNon-compliance with the additional obligations relating to children
₹150 croreFailure of a Significant Data Fiduciary to meet its additional obligations
₹50 croreAny other breach of the provisions of the Act or the Rules

The Board doesn't apply these maximums automatically — it weighs how serious the breach was, what kind of data was involved, whether it's happened before, what the organisation gained or avoided losing, and what it did to fix things afterward. In practice, that means a well-documented, good-faith compliance effort can genuinely bring the number down, even after something has gone wrong.
